# Chapter 3 — The colonial conquest

Berlin, November 15, 1884. In Chancellor Bismarck's residence on Wilhelmstrasse, representatives of fourteen European powers and the United States take their seats around a table. On the wall, a map of Africa — vast, and largely empty in the eyes of those looking at it: most of these diplomats have never seen the territories they are about to divide among themselves. Their hands do not tremble. The millions of men and women their pencils are tracing over have no names to them.

The conference will run until February 26, 1885. For the first time in the history of world diplomacy, an international meeting is devoted exclusively to a continent that is not represented at it. Not a single African in the room. Africa is carved into zones of influence, its rivers opened to European navigation, its coastlines turned into anchor points for systematic penetration inland. This is what is politely called the "Scramble for Africa." It is the founding crime of the modern world order.

## A surgical operation on the African body

The Berlin Congress is a surgical operation on the African body — performed without the patient, and without anesthesia. The borders drawn in Berlin ignore preexisting ethnic, linguistic, and political realities: the empires, kingdoms, and networks of alliances described in the previous chapters are cut through, sliced up, scattered among several European "possessions."

More than a century later, the verdict is delivered even from the podium of the French National Assembly, by MP André Chassaigne:

> *"They pay no attention to the community spaces that exist. They split up families, clans, communities. This still has consequences today."*

This surgery on maps would produce a century and a half of conflict — which the same powers would later present as proof of Africans' incapacity to govern themselves. This mechanism deserves close attention, because it is the template for everything this book will tell. The lie is complete, circular, self-referential: the problem is created, presented as proof of the natural inequality of peoples, and one imposes oneself as the solution to the problem one created.

A people is cut in two by an arbitrary border; the border is left to produce tensions; and then those tensions are pointed at as proof that "those people" clearly need to be administered. The arsonist sets the fire, then bills for putting it out. Readers will find this mechanism at every stage of this book — one need only replace "borders" with "armed groups" for the formula to remain exact.

## The rules of the game: "effective occupation"

The General Act signed in Berlin fixes a decisive rule among Europeans, known as "effective occupation": for a power to be able to claim an African territory against its rivals, it is no longer enough to plant a flag there — it must actually occupy it, install an administration, and station an armed force.

This clause turns the theoretical carve-up into a race against time: every chancellery now has an interest in sending its military columns as far and as fast as possible, ahead of the others. This is what explains the blistering brutality of the following two decades — what historians would call the "Scramble for Africa."

Note the elegance of the arrangement: Berlin does not organize war; Berlin organizes peace… among Europeans. The fourteen powers agree not to kill one another by dividing up the spoils. The violence, meanwhile, is entirely offloaded onto those who are not at the table.

All of it under a humanitarian flag. The acts of the conference invoke the fight against the slave trade and the "moral and material well-being" of native populations. Leopold II, King of the Belgians, thus secures recognition of his "Congo Free State" in the name of philanthropy and science — before turning it into one of the deadliest exploitation regimes of the modern era. The technique deserves to be remembered, for it never leaves the toolbox: from the "civilizing mission" of 1885 to the "counterterrorism" interventions of the twenty-first century, domination never advances without a moral pretext.

## The micro-state trap

Some Africans saw the trap very early. Barthélemy Boganda, founder of the Central African Republic — who died in 1959 in a plane crash under suspicious circumstances — and Kwame Nkrumah, father of Ghanaian independence, warned of the same threat: nonviable micro-states, condemned to permanent dependency, incapable of carrying individual weight in an international system built against them.

A state that is too small, landlocked, carved up with no economic logic, can neither defend itself alone, nor negotiate alone, nor develop alone: it is structurally condemned to seek a protector — and the obvious protector will be the former colonizer. Berlin 1884–1885 had manufactured the condition of impossible sovereignty. This was not an unforeseen consequence. It was the plan.

The fate of the two men adds to the warning: Boganda dies before his country's independence, in circumstances never fully clarified, and Nkrumah is overthrown in 1966. The federations they called for would not come into being in their lifetimes. It would take until 2023 and the Liptako-Gourma Charter for an institutional response to this fragmentation to emerge in the Sahel — three states born of the Berlin carve-up deciding, one hundred and forty years later, to pool their sovereignty.

## The OCRS law and the case of Sahelian resources

Even before independence was granted, France had already organized the securing of what really mattered. The law of January 10, 1957 created the Organisation Commune des Régions Sahariennes (OCRS) — a special status that administratively detached the desert zones rich in mineral resources from the colonies about to become independent. The subsoil of present-day Niger — its uranium — and its neighbors were to remain under French management, regardless of who governed the surface. Put plainly: give them the flags, we keep the oil and the mines.

This text would be explicitly cited from the podium of the United Nations by the Burkinabé representative Bassolma Bazié in 2025, to explain that Sahelian dependency is not a historical accident. It was built precisely and legally, by an act of the French state, before formal decolonization even took place.

## Before Berlin, after Berlin

The scale of the shift must be measured. Before Berlin, Europeans were present on African coasts through trading posts; explorations into the interior were only beginning. After Berlin, it is systematic conquest: treaties extracted from African sovereigns by threat or by deceit, military expeditions, forced labor, burned villages.

In Leopold II's Congo, the hands of workers who failed to deliver their rubber quota are cut off — the episode is documented even in the European archives of the time. Serious estimates put the death toll at 10 million between 1885 and 1908 in the Congo Free State alone. Ten million. For rubber. Pan-Africanist activist Banda Kani draws from this an unsparing formula:

> *"Every crime is permitted: inexplicable killings, genocide, murder in broad daylight, the cutting off of hands, feet, and ears, ritual crime, beheading, deportation, the pouring of napalm."*
> — Banda Kani, Paroles Panafricanistes, November 29, 2022

One can debate the tone. One cannot debate the overall logic: the entire arsenal of terror is deployed toward a single end — installing fear as the governing principle of an entire continent.

Because governing is exactly what this is. Colonial violence is not an excess: it is a method. It aims to break existing political structures — the empires, the sultanates, the charters described in the first two chapters — and replace them with an administration whose only legitimacy is force. The old center collapses; African societies, stripped of their institutions, are thrown back onto their particularisms. The fruits of this fragmentation will be harvested for a century and a half — and it will be called "African instability." The mechanics are cynical. They are also devastatingly effective.

## Algeria, laboratory of colonial terror

Berlin in 1884 merely organized among European powers a method already tested for half a century on Algerian soil. An officer of the French expeditionary force, landing in 1830, describes a land that the metropole had presented as "wild and uninhabited," which he finds covered with "pretty country houses surrounded by gardens," lush vegetation, abundant springs, and a network of aqueducts supplying the capital. Marshal Bugeaud himself acknowledges that Algeria produces substantial quantities of grain and livestock, and that the sheer extent of cultivated land points to a population far larger than announced. Commander Claude-Antoine Rozet, in his *Voyage dans la Régence d'Alger* (1833), notes that nearly all the men can read and count — while France of the same period counts roughly 40% illiteracy. The soldiers who land to "civilize" are, on average, less educated than the populations they have come to subdue.

The lie of the empty, savage land, already documented for the Sahel in the previous chapter, finds here its Algerian version — and the archives themselves, once again, disprove it.

What follows leaves no room for ambiguity. War Minister Girard writes that "we must resign ourselves to driving back, even exterminating, the native population [...] devastation, arson, the ruin of agriculture are perhaps the only means of firmly establishing our domination." The Duke of Rovigo orders his men to "bring back heads" and to "stop up water pipes with the head of the first Bedouin you meet." In April 1832, a detachment out of Algiers surprises the disarmed El Ouffia tribe and massacres men, women, and children indiscriminately — roughly 12,000 dead by some estimates; the seized herds are sold to Denmark's consul, and the rest of the plunder is displayed at the Bab Azoun market: bracelets still attached to severed wrists, earrings hanging from strips of flesh.

Colonel de Montagnac states the method without euphemism: "All populations that do not accept our conditions must be razed [...] grass must never grow again where the French army has set foot." Future marshal Saint-Arnaud, in a letter to his wife, describes in the tone of domestic small talk the planned burning of twenty villages that refuse to pay the tax he has imposed on them. In June 1845, the Ouled Riah tribe, driven out by Colonel Pélissier's burning columns, takes refuge in mountain caves. Bugeaud's order leaves no room for interpretation: "Smoke them out relentlessly, like foxes." A French witness describes the scene: the groans of men, women, children, and animals, the collapse of scorched rock; by morning, 760 corpses, human and animal intertwined, including a man found dead on his knees, hand clenched on an ox's horn, having tried to the last to shield his family. On May 8 of that same year, Saint-Arnaud discovers 500 Algerians sheltering in a cave between Ténès and Mostaganem and orders every exit sealed: "I am making a vast cemetery. The earth will forever cover the corpses of these fanatics."

Montagnac, again, sums up the doctrine of war: "Kill all men down to the age of fifteen, take all the women and children [...] some are kept as hostages, the rest are traded for horses, and the remainder sold at auction like pack animals" — boasting, in the same letter, of "a full basket of ears collected" from prisoners. Questioned in the Chamber of Deputies about these practices, Marshal Soult, the war minister, answers without flinching: "In Europe, such an act would be horrible and detestable. In Africa, it is simply war." Civilization, even then, kept two separate moralities.

The contrast with the Algerian adversary completes the case. Emir Abd el-Kader issued a decree guaranteeing a reward to any Arab who brought in a French soldier alive, and requiring that any French prisoner be well treated and promptly brought before the caliph or the emir himself — on pain of forfeiting the reward. Asked about the relative value of a severed head versus a living prisoner, the emir answered that the latter earned a reward in gold; the former, twenty-five strokes of the cane on the soles of the feet. Which side, in truth, held civilization?

These quotations and period engravings, preserved at France's national library, later formed the basis of a documentary offered, decades on, to France's Ligue de l'enseignement for screening in secondary schools. The written refusal is worth quoting in full for what it reveals about the education system itself: "Although very correctly produced and based on indisputable documents, this film runs counter to all the ideas underpinning the teaching of the French penetration of North Africa, which makes its distribution impossible." The admission is total: the teaching of colonial history was never meant to open a window onto documented reality, but to indoctrinate in the service of a political idea — colonialism itself, protected by the very institution meant to transmit its critical memory.

## The letter of September 13, 1898

Behind the civilizing rhetoric, colonial archives sometimes yield documents that make commentary unnecessary. On September 13, 1898, a French governor signs an instruction to his officers operating in French Sudan — present-day Mali. The order is unambiguous: destroy the populations that resist, **poison the wells**, eliminate anything that could constitute a center of resistance. An officer of the same period notes that "*aviation will one day come and eliminate them all*" — projecting into the future a logic of extermination already present in the moment.

This letter, preserved in French archives, has never been cited in the Republic's history textbooks. Choguel Maïga, the Malian prime minister, personally cites it in 2024, holds a copy of it, and recalls its context: Malian populations hiding in the forests, concealing their livestock under mosquito nets, dying as they fled toward Guinea or Algeria — some of them members of his own family. *"Are they their friends? They are the friends of their own interests,"* he concludes.

This instruction is not an aberration. It is policy. It belongs to a decade in which resistance to French columns in French Sudan is systematically answered with the destruction of villages, the burning of crops, and the assassination of chiefs. This is no secret — it is an administrative choice that Paris covered, financed, and whose authors it promoted. The same logic — starve, isolate, terrorize in order to rule — reappears two centuries later in ECOWAS sanctions, fuel embargoes, and pressure on currencies. The instruments change. The logic remains.

## Côte d'Ivoire, 1949–1950: repression and its balance sheet

Colonial terror does not stop in the nineteenth century. Breton filmmaker René Vautier, a former resistance fighter turned documentarian, recorded it in 1950 in a film titled *Afrique 50* — commissioned by the French Ligue de l'enseignement, censored by the French state for more than forty years before finally being recognized in 1996. Sent to film "the reality of the colonies" with no prior briefing, Vautier found on the ground militants of the African Democratic Rally (RDA), founded in 1946 by former riflemen back from the Second World War — men who had fought Nazi racism only to come home to colonial racism. He chose to film alongside them, against the colonial authorities, in direct violation of a 1934 decree — signed by Pierre Laval, then colonial minister, executed in 1945 for collaboration with Nazi Germany — requiring that no film be shot in French West Africa without a pre-approved script and the presence of an official representative.

The film first documents colonialism's balance sheet. While the Compagnie française de l'Afrique occidentale and the Compagnie du Niger français posted, in 1949, profits of 650 and 365 million francs respectively from peanut fields worked by African women and children, a dam on the Niger River — the Markala dam — supplied electricity to European neighborhoods while its floodgates, serving the barges of the big colonial companies, were still operated by hand: African labor paid fifty francs a day cost less than maintaining a turbine. Forced labor, officially abolished in French Africa in 1946, continued in disguised form — the obligation to pay colonial tax in cash forced men to work for the companies at whatever rate they set.

Refusing to pay that tax carried a precisely documented price. On February 27, 1949, at five in the morning, colonial troops surrounded the village of Palaka in northern Côte d'Ivoire, whose chief had been unable to raise the demanded 3,700 francs. They opened fire, burned, killed. The village chief, Ali Ouattara, was shot in the back of the neck. A seven-month-old infant was killed, his skull shattered by a bullet. A pregnant woman died from two bullets to the stomach. The official toll lists four dead; African archives count considerably more.

Repression spread as the RDA mobilized: Agboville, Dimbokro, Séguéla, Daloa, Bouaflé — entire towns reduced to ruins under colonial gunfire, the same year France commemorated the martyrs of Oradour-sur-Glane, exterminated by the Nazis, without acknowledging any equivalence with what it was itself inflicting in Africa. Between October 1949 and February 1950, more than 400 Africans stood trial in Abidjan in the largest political trial ever held in Black Africa — women, the elderly, and children among them. During one peaceful demonstration, a colonial commander ordered troops to charge the crowd; fourteen Africans were killed, more than fifty wounded, no Europeans hurt. An investigation on the ground by a Secours populaire lawyer later established that it was not colonial troops who fired on the demonstrators that day, but armed European settlers, kept for weeks in a state of manufactured panic — including the local justice of the peace, whose testimony before the inquiry commission betrayed, in the words recorded at the time, "nervousness" and a "guilty conscience." That same justice of the peace received a dismissal of all charges.

That is the stage set at Berlin: a continent denied, carved up, terrorized. But Chancellor Bismarck's maps had overlooked one variable — the peoples they were carving up were about to fight, weapons in hand, for decades.
